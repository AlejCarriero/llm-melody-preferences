Does an AI have taste in melodies?

We showed a language model, Claude Fable 5.2, hundreds of pairs of short melodies and asked which one it preferred. It has never heard a sound: it only saw note names like C4 E4 G4. Each question went to a fresh copy of the model with no memory of the others. The question was whether anything stable sits behind its answers.

Try it yourself

Be the subject

Do the model's task: pick between pairs of melodies, by ear or by reading the notes as it did. See how consistent you are, and how your taste compares. Start, about 6 minutes

Listen and rate

Hear the melodies the model liked most and least, in a random order, and rate each one from 1 to 5. The ranking stays hidden until the end. Start, about 6 minutes

What we found

90%of pairs: the same melody won whichever order it was shown in. Chance would give 50%.

720choices, each by a separate copy of the model, for about $2.

So the model's stated preferences are not noise. They form a consistent ranking, and the shape is clear:

Average preference score for each of the ten families (4 to 8 melodies each). Positive means preferred. Scores come from a Bradley-Terry fit to all 720 choices.

It liked melodies that take a short figure and move it around: recognisable, but changing. It disliked exact repetition most of all, and disliked pure randomness less. An idealised listener's sense of surprise predicts its choices well, with a preferred level in the middle of the range.

What this doesn't show

It doesn't show why. The model might be computing something like surprise as it reads, or it might be repeating how people write about music: drones are "boring", folk tunes are "pleasing". This design can't tell those apart, so we say only that its choices are predicted by a listener's surprise.

It doesn't show the model can hear. One person rated 24 of the melodies by ear. They agreed with the model about what is dull and what is noise, but rated the short loops much higher than the model did. Written down, a loop looks empty; played, it can sound like a groove.

Asked to predict its own choices, the model did well, which proves little. Its predictions matched its choices about as often as its choices matched each other, which is what you would see if predicting simply meant choosing again. And when asked in advance what would drive its choices, every copy expected a bias towards the first option. There was none.

"Same choice in both orders": chance is about 50%, or less for a model with a strong habit of picking one slot. "Ranking agrees": rank correlation over all 60 melodies, from +1 (identical) to -1 (reversed).

Gemini, from a different lab, has almost exactly Claude's taste. The models that agree less with Claude are also less consistent with themselves, and a ranking that is partly noise can't match anything closely. Allowing for each model's own noise, the four models with stable preferences agree with each other at roughly +0.6 to +1.0 (an exploratory estimate). DeepSeek mostly picked whichever melody came first, and its choices didn't pass our pre-set test for stable preferences, so we don't read its ranking.

Shared taste across labs is what you'd expect if these preferences come from the human writing the models were all trained on. It doesn't prove that.

Caveats: the four comparison models ran with thinking off or at its lowest setting, while Claude ran at its default (it rarely thought before choosing). DeepSeek ran on an 8-bit copy from a third-party host. Claude, but not the others, was also asked to predict its own choices.

How it was done

  1. 60 melodies of 24 notes on a five-note scale, in ten families from one repeated note to fully random.
  2. Each melody was paired with 12 others. Every pair was shown in both orders, so a habit of answering "A" couldn't pass for a preference.
  3. Each choice was a brand-new conversation with the model through its API, with no memory and no other instructions.
  4. The predictions and the analysis were written down and committed before the data came in, and every later change was recorded as a dated amendment.

Code, data, predictions and full reports are on GitHub, with instructions for repeating the experiment on any model.
